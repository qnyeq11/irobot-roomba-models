## Why the names look unfamiliar now

iRobot filed for bankruptcy, and ownership passed to Picea, the manufacturer that had been building the robots. The company has said it will operate as before, with no changes to existing models or support.

The visible change is the naming. The old i-, j- and s-series labels are being retired in favour of a tiered number system — 105, 205, 405, 505, 705 — with "Plus" and "Max" prefixes stacked on top. New models launched in July, then another pair arrived in September, and the result is a store page where three generations of naming conventions live side by side.

Practically, this matters for one reason: a lower number does not mean an older design. A Roomba Plus 415 is a current-generation machine with LiDAR navigation and a self-washing dock. An i-series Roomba on the same shelf is not. Don't assume the alphabet is chronological.

## The dock is what you're actually buying

Here's the pattern buried in that table. Two models sit at $699.99: the Plus 575 Combo and the Max 715 Vac. Same price, almost opposite machines. The 575 mops and washes its own pads. The 715 doesn't mop at all but pulls 30,000 Pa against the 575's roughly 22,000 Pa.

That's not a pricing error. It's iRobot telling you the dock and the mopping hardware cost real money, and that if you only want suction, you can have a lot of it without paying for a mop you'll never fill.

So work out what you're buying first. Everything from the Plus 415 upward includes an AutoWash dock that rinses mop pads at 167°F, dries them at 113°F, and empties debris into a sealed bag — up to three months of hands-free running on the newest models, with the Max 875 dock also auto-dispensing cleaning solution. Everything below that tier either empties the bin and nothing more, or, in the case of the 205 DustCompactor, skips the dock entirely and compacts debris onboard so you empty it less often.

If you have hard floors and you're honest with yourself about how often you'd hand-wash a mop pad, the AutoWash dock is the feature worth paying for. If you have mostly carpet, it isn't, and the Max 715 is the smarter buy at the same price as a mopping machine.

## Obstacle avoidance is the quiet cut-off

Only the two most expensive models in the current line carry PrecisionVision AI, iRobot's object-recognition system. The rest navigate with LiDAR alone.

LiDAR is good at mapping a room and cleaning in tidy rows. It is not good at spotting a phone charging cable, a sock, or a dog toy. It maps the floor plan beautifully and then runs straight over the mess. If your floors are usually clear, that's a non-issue. If you have kids, a shaggy pet, or a habit of leaving shoes wherever they land, budget for the Max 715 or Max 775 — or accept that you'll be doing a two-minute pickup before every run, which is roughly the amount of work the robot was supposed to save you.

Pet owners get a genuine win across the board, though. The newer models use tangle-resistant rubber brushes and a three-prong side brush, which matters more in daily use than any suction number. A robot that doesn't need its brush unthreaded once a week is worth more than one that pulls 5,000 extra pascal it doesn't need.

## What to actually buy

If you have mostly hard floors and want the least ongoing work: a Plus-tier Combo with an AutoWash dock. The Plus 415 covers the basics; the Plus 515 and 575 add runtime and suction for more money. You're paying for the dock, and it earns its keep.

If you have mostly carpet: skip the mopping models and take the Max 715 Vac. Same price as a mopping machine, considerably more suction, and you won't be maintaining a mop pad you never needed.

If your floors are cluttered or you own a shedder: the Max 775 Combo, or the Max 875 if you want the newest heated-mist mopping and the 35,000 Pa figure. This is the only tier where obstacle recognition is included, and for a lot of homes that single feature decides whether the robot gets used daily or gets forgotten in a cupboard.
